## PRIMARY TEXT 4 (long-form A): The Breakout

The typical American house is 42 years old now. (NAHB, 2026)

Carol's has it beat. And it keeps score.

Eleven marks on the hallway wall. One for every year she told the kids "next spring."

Sellers stay a record 11 years now. The typical one is 64. The oldest on record. (NAR, 2025)

She was doin' time in her own house. The roof was the warden. The stairs were the guard. Fourteen up, fourteen down, one hand on the rail.

The stain on the back bedroom ceiling was a dinner plate last winter. It's a serving platter now.

The contractor's estimate sat on the kitchen table until the second estimate landed on top of it. Then the third.

Every year she waited, the house turned a year older. The stairs never got shorter.

Plan A: fix it all, then list it. The ceiling had other ideas.

Plan B: clean it all out, then list it. The boxes in the garage had been "going to Goodwill" since the kids had kids. The boxes won.

Neither plan got her out. Both plans kept her in.

Then she tried the one way out she'd never tried. The front door.

The house sells as-is. The ceiling comes with it. No commission, no fees, no closing costs.

Truth? Cash offers usually come in lower than listing. Ours too.

But "higher" has a bill stapled to it. A listed sale means the roof, the ceiling, the clean-out, the showings, the commission, the closing costs, and staying put while it sits.

That's why lower isn't everything.

Carol made one call.

Nobody asked her to fix anything first. Nobody asked her to clean anything out.

She took what mattered. The albums. Her mother's cast iron. The good scissors.

She left the rest. Garage and all. The hallway wall stayed for the next warden.

She circled her own closing date on the kitchen calendar. In pen. Nobody circled it for her.

Sunday dinner is at Carol's now. One floor. Ten minutes from the grandkids, which is close enough that they show up unannounced and far enough that they go home.

Nobody counts stairs. There aren't any.

The ceiling is just a ceiling. She hasn't looked up at it once.

The albums live on a low shelf. The cast iron lives on the stove, where it gets used.

Saturdays are soccer games, not hardware stores. She knows the snack schedule. She does not know where the nearest roofer is, and she's not going to find out.

Last week her grandson stood against the new kitchen doorframe and she penciled in the first mark.

It's the only mark on that wall. It isn't keeping score. It's keeping track.

How many marks are on your hallway wall?

Ask for a cash offer. No obligation. Just don't tell the house.

## PRIMARY TEXT 5 (long-form B): Visiting Hours

Insurance on the typical American home is up 24% in three years. (Consumer Federation of America)

Carol's renewal came in a window envelope. It went on the pile with the other window envelopes.

That's how she tracked the years in that house. Not a calendar. A pile.

Visiting hours were Sundays, two to five. The kids drove an hour each way to sit in a kitchen with a stain on the ceiling and a bucket under it.

The roof was the warden. The stairs were the guard. The garage was solitary. Nobody went in there anymore.

Plan A: fix it all, then list it. The roofer's estimate joined the pile.

Plan B: clean it all out, then list it. The garage filed an appeal.

And first-time buyers are the smallest share on record. 21%. (NAR, 2025) Carol pictured the open house. Cookies on the counter. Nobody coming up the walk.

Every year she waited, the house got a year older. The pile got taller. The visits got shorter.

Then she tried the door everybody else used. The front one.

The house sells as-is. Bucket and all. No commission, no fees, no closing costs.

Truth? Cash offers usually come in lower than listing. Ours too.

Plan A's estimate and Plan B's garage had already told her what "higher" costs.

That's why lower isn't everything.

Nobody asked her to get the house ready first. It wasn't. It sold as it was.

Carol took what mattered. The albums. The cast iron. The pile went in the recycling.

She left the rest. Garage, bucket, stain.

She circled her own closing date. Nobody picked it for her. She picked a Tuesday, because she could.

Visiting hours are over. Carol lives ten minutes from the kids now, on one floor, and she's the one who drops by.

Sunday dinner moved to her place. Nobody drives an hour. Nobody looks up. Nobody brings a bucket.

The mail comes one envelope at a time, and she opens it.

Saturdays are soccer games. She brings the orange slices and sits in the sun. Nobody at the game asks about the roof. There is a roof. It's somebody else's.

Her granddaughter stood against the new doorframe and got the first pencil mark. One mark. Nothing to count.

What year was your house built?

Ask for a cash offer. No obligation.
