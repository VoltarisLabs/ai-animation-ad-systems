# David Ogilvy

**Who:** Founder of Ogilvy & Mather. Called the "Father of Advertising."
**Key works:** *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (1963), *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983).
**Core idea:** Advertising exists to sell. Research comes before writing, and the headline carries most of the load.

## 1. Frameworks
- **Headline-first economics.** About five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. So the headline is where most of the ad's money is spent. If the headline does not sell, most of the budget is wasted.
- **The Big Idea.** An ad has to rest on one concept strong enough to carry a campaign for years. Execution cannot save a weak idea.
- **Research, then write.** Study the product, the competitors and the consumer before writing anything. Many of his headlines came from product facts he found by digging.
- **Brand image.** Every ad adds to, or takes away from, the long-term personality of the brand.

## 2. Actionable rules
1. Put the benefit in the headline. Say what the reader gets.
2. Put the brand name in the headline, because most people never read the body.
3. Specific facts beat general claims. A measurable detail is more believable than an adjective.
4. Long copy sells when the product needs explaining and the reader is interested. Readers who are not interested were never going to buy.
5. People read captions under photos more than body copy. Every photo needs a caption that sells.
6. News in the headline works: "new," "introducing," "announcing," or a real product change.
7. Write to one person, in plain language. Paraphrasing his well-known line: the consumer is not a moron, so don't talk down to them.
8. Test everything: headlines, offers, media.
9. Don't be clever at the cost of clarity. Obscure or punning headlines lose readers.
10. Do not bury the promise under brand chatter.

## 3. Psychological triggers
- **Specificity = credibility.** Exact numbers and concrete details lower skepticism.
- **Story appeal.** A strange or unexplained detail in the image makes people want the story behind it.
- **Authority and prestige.** He tied products to premium, trusted cues.
- **Curiosity** from an unresolved visual or headline detail.

## 4. Examples and teardowns
- **Rolls-Royce (1958):** "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock." This is a single, checkable engineering fact (he credited it to a technical report) that stands in for the whole idea of "quiet luxury." Lesson: dramatize one real fact instead of stacking adjectives.
- **Hathaway shirt man:** A model wore an eye patch that was never explained. The unexplained detail gave the ads "story appeal" and made the brand stand out. Lesson: an oddity in the image can get attention without the copy asking for it.

## Apply it (paid ads)
- Test 5 to 10 headlines before touching body copy or visuals.
- Replace every adjective in a draft with a fact: "fast" becomes "delivered in 48 hours" or "set up in 11 minutes."
