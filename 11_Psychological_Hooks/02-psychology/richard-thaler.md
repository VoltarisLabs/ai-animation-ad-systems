# Richard Thaler

**Who:** Won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2017. Professor at the University of Chicago.
**Key works:** *Nudge* (2008, with Cass Sunstein), *Misbehaving* (2015).
**Core idea:** How choices are presented (choice architecture) steers decisions without taking any options away.

## 1. Frameworks
- **Choice architecture / nudges.** Defaults, order, framing and grouping change what people choose, while every option stays available.
- **Defaults.** Most people stick with the pre-selected option.
- **Mental accounting.** People put money into separate mental buckets (bills, fun money, windfall) and treat each bucket differently.
- **Endowment effect.** People value something more once they own it.
- **Transaction utility.** People enjoy "a good deal" apart from what they actually bought. The price compared to the expected price matters.
- **Sludge.** Friction that works against the person (hard cancellation, hidden steps). It is the opposite of a nudge.

## 2. Actionable rules
1. Pre-select the recommended option (plan, package, time slot) in a way the buyer can easily change.
2. Reframe the price into a smaller mental account ("about $2 a day") or match it to an existing account (insurance claim, tax refund).
3. Create a sense of ownership early: free trial, "your custom quote," a personalized report.
4. Show the expected or regular price so a deal feels like a deal.
5. Remove sludge from your own funnel. Friction lowers conversion and trust.
6. Nudge ethically: steer toward options that are actually good for the buyer.

## 3. Key studies
- **Mug experiment (Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, 1990):** People given a mug asked for about twice as much to sell it as others would pay to buy one.
- **Save More Tomorrow (Thaler & Benartzi, 2004):** Committing *future* raises to savings made savings rates rise sharply. It worked because it avoided felt loss now.
- **Retirement auto-enrollment:** Making enrollment the default greatly increased participation compared with opt-in.
