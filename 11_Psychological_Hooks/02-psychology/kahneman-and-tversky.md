# Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky

**Who:** Founders of behavioral economics. Kahneman won the 2002 Nobel Prize in Economics (Tversky died in 1996, before it was awarded). Kahneman died in 2024.
**Key works:** "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases" (1974), "Prospect Theory" (1979), *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (Kahneman, 2011).
**Core idea:** People decide mostly with fast intuition, and that intuition has predictable biases.

## 1. Frameworks
- **System 1 vs. System 2.** System 1 is fast, automatic and emotional. System 2 is slow, effortful and logical. Most ads are judged by System 1 in a fraction of a second.
- **Prospect Theory.** People judge outcomes as gains or losses from a reference point, not as final amounts. Losses weigh roughly twice as much as equal gains (**loss aversion**).
- **Heuristics:**
  - **Anchoring:** The first number seen pulls later estimates toward it.
  - **Availability:** What comes to mind easily seems more common or likely.
  - **Representativeness:** Judging by resemblance to a stereotype, ignoring base rates.
- **Framing.** The same facts described differently lead to different choices.
- **Peak-End Rule.** Experiences are remembered by their most intense moment and their end, not their average.
- **Cognitive ease.** Statements that are easy to read, familiar or repeated are more likely to be believed.
- **WYSIATI** ("What You See Is All There Is"). People decide from the information in front of them and rarely ask what is missing.

## 2. Actionable rules
1. Frame the cost of inaction as a loss ("every month you wait costs $X") when accurate.
2. Anchor high first: show the full or competitor price before yours.
3. Use a vivid, recent, local example to make a risk feel real (availability).
4. Make copy easy to process: high contrast, simple words, short lines. Ease reads as truth.
5. Design the end of every funnel carefully. The last step shapes how the whole experience is remembered.
6. Put everything needed to decide inside the ad. People do not go looking for missing facts.

## 3. Key studies
- **Wheel of fortune anchoring (1974):** A rigged wheel landing on 10 or 65 shifted people's estimates of the percentage of UN members that are African countries (median answers of 25% vs. 45%).
- **"Asian disease" framing (1981):** Identical outcomes framed as "lives saved" or "lives lost" flipped the majority choice.
- **Colonoscopy (Redelmeier & Kahneman, 1996; trial by Redelmeier, Katz & Kahneman, 2003):** Patients' memories tracked the worst moment and the end, not the length. In the 2003 trial, adding a short, milder period at the end made patients rate the whole procedure as less unpleasant.

## Evidence note
Loss aversion, anchoring and framing are among the best-replicated findings in the field, although the "2x" loss ratio varies by context. The social priming studies in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (chapter 4) did not replicate well, and Kahneman publicly acknowledged relying on them too heavily. Don't build ads on priming claims.
