# Sell the destination, not the plane

Applies to every script in this repo, every format: skeleton, crochet, singing, claymation, talking objects, UGC, game-look (`/gta-style`), statics. The [claims registry](claims-registry.md) still wins over everything here: this file decides what a script spends its time on, the registry decides which words are allowed.

## The idea

People don't buy a product, a company or a process. They buy progress in their own life. Theodore Levitt: nobody wants a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole. Clayton Christensen turned that into Jobs to Be Done: customers "hire" a product to make progress.

Brian Tracy's version, from a sales talk: if you sell a trip to the Canary Islands, talk 90% about the islands (palm trees, warm water) and 10% about the plane. Most ads do the opposite: the seat map, the food, the movie. (The talk also says 15% of buying is driven by the past and 85% by the future. No published source for those numbers was found. Treat them as sales folklore and never cite them as research.)

## What the research adds (deep dive, 2026-10-03)

The idea holds, with one correction that changes how we write: **an ad that shows only the outcome persuades less than one that also shows the customer's own easy steps toward it.**

- **Picturing the steps drives action more than picturing the result.** Escalas and Luce had people imagine using a product (process) or imagine its benefits (outcome). Process led to higher purchase intent, because people start planning in their heads.
- **Ads that show steps are more believable than before/after ads.** Cian, Longoni and Krishna (Journal of Marketing Research, 2020): "progression" ads that show steps in between beat before/after ads on credibility and persuasion, because that is how change happens in real life. **Exception:** when people want the result fast and fast is possible, showing a process backfires. Keep the steps few and easy.
- **Near decisions weigh "can I do it", far decisions weigh "do I want it".** Construal-level research (Liberman and Trope): desirability dominates distant decisions, feasibility dominates near ones. Travel promotions follow the same pattern: abstract messages for distant trips, concrete ones for near trips (Kim et al., 2016). A seller deciding this month needs more of the path.
- **"Imagine..." backfires when it's hard to imagine.** Petrova and Cialdini (2005): imagery appeals lowered preference when the picture was hard to form or the product wasn't shown vividly.
- **A story about a character persuades with less pushback.** Escalas: being absorbed in a story (narrative transportation) lowers counterarguing, and weak arguments stop hurting.
- **The effect is real but small, and how you run it matters.** Ceylan, Diehl and Wood (2024 meta-analysis, 237 effects, 40,705 people): imagined use moves behavior a little. It grows with moving visuals plus words together, and with repeats spaced over time. The same message everywhere at once (or heavy retargeting of one ad) lowers it.
- **The destination is one lever of four.** Hormozi's value equation: value = (dream outcome x how likely they believe it is) / (time delay x effort). Speed is blocked by the registry, so cutting effort ("the house sells as-is", "take what matters, leave the rest") is our biggest approved lever after the destination itself. Effort cuts are PATH, not PLANE.

## The four tags

| Tag | What it is | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **PAIN** | The problem in their life, told through a character | Earns the scroll |
| **PLANE** | How the company works: offer mechanics, the company, features, process described from our side | One line, or none |
| **PATH** | The customer's own easy steps, from their side: asks for the offer, carries out the one box that matters, circles a date, locks the door one last time | 1 to 3 short beats, never a long process |
| **DESTINATION** | Their life after: where they are, who they're with, what they get to do next | The payoff. One concrete scene |

## Budget (share of spoken words; tag the "What we see" cells too)

| Ad | PAIN | PLANE | PATH | DESTINATION |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold, unaware | ~35% | 10% or less | ~20% | 35% or more |
| Hot: deciding now (a listing that's sat, a seller who already clicked, retargeting) | ~30% | 10% or less | ~30% | ~30% |

## How it shows up in a script

1. **The mechanism gets one line.** "The house was never the problem. The loan was." is enough.
2. **Every approved feature line gets a picture,** either as a step on her path or as her life after. Keep the registry wording; put the rest on screen:

   | Approved line | Show it as PATH | Or as DESTINATION |
   |---|---|---|
   | "The house sells as-is." | She locks the front door one last time, tarp still on the roof | Saturday morning on a new porch, no ladder in sight |
   | "Take what matters. Leave the rest." | She carries out one framed photo; the boxes stay | That photo on a shelf in the new place |
   | "No commission. No closing costs." | She signs at a calm kitchen table | The same table, no stack of bills |
   | "You pick the closing date." | She circles a date on the calendar | That week: the grandkids running through the new place |
   | "Ask for a cash offer. No obligation." | She makes one call | (end card) |

3. **Few, easy steps.** One to three path beats. A long process reads like effort and triggers the quick-fix backfire.
4. **The last content beat shows the next chapter, not just the problem ending.** "MISSION COMPLETE" says the pain stopped; "NEW AREA UNLOCKED" (a new place on the map, the homeowner with family) shows what they bought.
5. **Easy to picture.** One person, one place, one action. Never an abstract word ("freedom", "peace of mind") and never "imagine..." over a vague outcome.
6. **Tell it through a character, in third person.** It persuades better and it is the platform-safe way to show the pain (`AD_COPY_CHECKLIST.md` Step 8).
7. **Words and pictures together.** The narrator names the destination and the screen shows it, at the same moment.
8. **The Ask hands the destination to the viewer.** Put the viewer in the hero's place on screen (their own house marker, the same win landing on it). The spoken Ask stays registry wording: "Ask for a cash offer. No obligation."

## Running the ads

- **Space the repeats.** Rotate destination variants week to week. Don't launch one ad on every placement at once, and don't retarget a single ad hard.
- **Test path against destination.** Same hook, two cuts: one heavier on path, one heavier on destination. Log which wins for which audience in [swipe-file.md](swipe-file.md).

## Check before any script ships

- [ ] Every spoken line and every "What we see" cell is tagged PAIN, PLANE, PATH or DESTINATION.
- [ ] PLANE is 10% or less of the spoken words and said once.
- [ ] One to three PATH beats, all from the customer's side.
- [ ] DESTINATION meets the budget for the audience (35%+ cold, ~30% hot).
- [ ] The destination is one concrete scene (person, place, action), not an abstract word.
- [ ] The last image before the end card is their next chapter.
- [ ] Told through a character, in third person.
- [ ] Every line passes the [claims registry](claims-registry.md). Path and destination pictures add no claim: no money amounts, no speed or timeline, no "we buy".

## Worked example (game-look, rates angle, Oct 2026)

First draft, tagged: PAIN 40%, PLANE 45% ("a buyer who doesn't need a loan", the fee list, "no appraiser, no bank"), PATH 0%, DESTINATION about 3 seconds (a sunset). The rewrite cut the turn to one line ("The house was never the problem. The loan was."), added two path beats (she locks the door one last time; she circles a date) and gave the closes destination pictures (a calm table with no bills, then the map pulls back and a new area lights up with the homeowner on a porch with her grandkids). Spoken closes stayed in registry wording.
