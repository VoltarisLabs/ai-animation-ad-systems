### 1. Meta Model vs Milton Model (00:01:49 - 00:04:06)

- **Meta Model** (older therapy): precise, confronting questions that force specifics.
  "I am unhappy." → "Unhappy about what, specifically?"
  "My partner ignores me, so they don't love me." → "How specifically does not answering lead you to conclude they don't love you?"
  Makes the person think consciously → resistance.
- **Milton Model** (Milton H. Erickson, codified by Bandler & Grinder): **purposely vague**. Strip the specifics so the listener fills them in from their own life.
  "You already know what's been holding you back…"
- **Transderivational search**: the brain searches its own memories to find a match for vague input. An idea the viewer produced themselves feels far more true than one you tell them (00:04:08 - 00:04:45).
- **Self-referencing** (00:15:54): if an idea came from inside the person, they treat it as fact.

### 2. Three Milton patterns for copy (00:04:45 - 00:06:12)

| Pattern | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Unspecified nouns/verbs | Viewer fills in their own | "Something has to change." |
| Cause-effect | Links a true thing to a suggestion | "As you watch this, you'll start to notice…" |
| Presupposition | Smuggles the premise past scrutiny | "Before you decide *which* size to order…" (not *whether*) |

### 3. Nested loops structure (00:07:08 - 00:12:10)

```
Open Loop A   (the hook: the reason they stopped)
  Open Loop B
    Open Loop C
      *PAYLOAD*  (unique mechanism, authority, emotional story, identity gap)
    Close C
  Close B
Close A       (closed LAST)
→ short, simple CTA
```

- Loops close in **reverse order**. The hook loop (A) closes last, because by then the mechanism is explained and A is easy to pay off (00:29:14).
- **Updated slide (edited live in Canva, 00:23:02 - 00:24:00, slide 22).** Payload is not only in the middle. He added *PAYLOAD* between every step:

```text
Open Loop A
  *PAYLOAD*
Open Loop B
  *PAYLOAD*
Open Loop C
  *PAYLOAD*
Close Loop C
  *PAYLOAD*
Close Loop B
  *PAYLOAD*
Close Loop A
CTA
```

  Meaning: never go from one loop line straight to the next. Each gap carries content: story, proof, emotion, mechanism.
- Variations allowed: open A, open B, close A, open C, open D, close B… Rule: never open one and close it right away. Always close every loop (00:16:44).

#### Loop order rule (user rule, 2026-09-24; matches the call at 00:16:44-00:17:08)

**Allowed: two patterns**

```text
Nested:     Open A → Open B → Open C → payload → Close C → Close B → Close A
Staggered:  Open A → Open B → Close A → Open C → Close B → Close C
```

- Always keep at least one other loop open when you close one.
- Always open a new loop before the last one closes.

**Forbidden: open and close back to back**

```text
Open A → Close A → Open B → Close B → Open C → Close C
```

- This does not work, because nothing is ever left hanging.
- Never open a loop and close it on the next beat.

**Staggered pattern:** save the biggest payoff (the hook's promise) for the **last** close, whatever letter it has.
- Sweet spot **3-4 loops open, max 5** (00:28:55). Close a minor loop within about 1 minute; the hook loop can stay open to the end (00:36:10).
- Loops ride inside a **story**: "stories within stories". The payload lands as part of the story, not as a claim (00:11:46).

### 4. Why he says it works (00:08:24 - 00:16:17)

- **Zeigarnik effect**: unfinished questions sit in working memory.
- **Working memory limit**: he cites ~7 items (Miller). Open loops fill it → less room for analysis → payload gets less scrutiny.
- **Closure**: closing C→B→A gives a sense of completion and coherence. Viewer does not go back and audit the payload (00:11:03).
- **Narrative transportation**: good story → viewer loses track of time. Why 10-minute VSLs can work.
- **Emotional engagement**: empathy, joy, sadness with the characters.
- **Mental imagery**: use very visual language.
- **Cognitive load reduces scrutiny**: viewer falls back on shortcuts (availability heuristic, fit into existing schemas; easy-to-understand = feels true).

### 5. Worked example: "edible deodorant" (spoken 00:21:26; slide 23 built live 00:30:10 - 00:35:20)

The finished slide, word for word:

```text
edible deodorant ─────────────────────────────── Open A
  The deodorant industry is lying to you ──────── Open B
    Your BO doesn't come from your skin ───────── Open C
      *PAYLOAD*
    That's why deodorant doesn't work, because
    bo is coming from the gut ─────────────────── Close C
  That's why the deodorant brands charge you
  10 bucks ────────────────────────────────────── Close B
This is [product] ─────────────────────────────── Close A
CTA
```

What each line does:

| Line | Job | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| A: "edible deodorant" | Stops the scroll. Two words that should not go together. | Juxtaposition (curiosity) |
| B: "…industry is lying to you" | Names a villain. Viewer asks "how?" | Contrarian / enemy |
| C: "Your BO doesn't come from your skin" | Breaks a belief the viewer holds. Viewer asks "then where?" | Belief break |
| Payload | Explains the new mechanism: odor starts in the gut | Unique mechanism, story, authority |
| Close C: "That's why… because bo is coming from the gut" | Answers C with the mechanism | "That's why" + real "because" |
| Close B: "That's why the brands charge you 10 bucks" | Answers B: the villain's motive (profit) | "That's why" |
| Close A: "This is [product]" | Answers A: the product IS the edible deodorant | Product reveal |
| CTA | One short action | - |

Two things to copy:
1. **Every close starts with "That's why…".** It ties the payload back to the claim the viewer is still holding. It is the cause-effect pattern from section 2.
2. **The product name is saved for the last close.** The hook's promise ("what is an edible deodorant?") is only paid off when the product appears.

### 6. Other rules from the Q&A

- **Length**: full nested loops need ~2-2.5 min+. A 30-second ad has no time for A/B/C (00:25:08). Short ads: the hook open loop is still the key piece (00:19:52).
- **Vague vs specific** (00:26:36): be **specific** in the hook and first lines, in the avatar's own words, to call out the right person. Go **vague** where you want each viewer to feel the emotion in their own way.
- **Soft CTA** can work: end on "this has changed my life, I'm going to give it to all my friends": the platform link does the clicking (00:17:30).
- **Modeling winners** (00:38:04): copy a mega-winner's hook structure; copying the lead only and changing the body failed for him; copying the whole structure worked.
- **Visuals explain the mechanism** (00:43:28): same VSL went from $3K/day to $17K/day after new animations that explained the mechanism better. (His claim, not verified.)
- Close with identity: identity discrepancy (gap between who they are and who they want to be) → product closes the gap (00:51:25).

### 6b. Working Memory Model (Google image shown 00:46:40 and 00:51:55 - 00:53:09; source on screen: "The Working Memory Model (Baddeley and Hitch, 1974) - A Simple Summary | The IB Psychology Blog")

```text
                 Central Executive
        ┌───────────────┼────────────────┐
 Phonological Loop   Episodic Buffer   Visuo-Spatial Scratchpad
   ┌──────┴──────┐
Articulatory   Acoustic
   Loop         Store
```

What he said:
- **Central executive:** "the control system… very limited capacity… doesn't store anything itself, its job is to decide where the attention goes."
- **Phonological loop:** "deals with sound and speech-based information". It splits into the articulatory loop (rehearsal) and the acoustic store (the sounds). Information "is held for about two seconds… then your brain has to rehearse".
- **Visuo-spatial scratchpad:** "just like the mental image".
- **Game analogy (47:33):** you keep in mind that a player on the map is moving left. That is holding something in working memory while doing other things.
- **Link to loops (48:07):** open loops keep the phonological loop busy, so "there's not enough storage for them to keep remembering… the very emotional copy goes straight to the subconscious."

Corrections (see Part B 4f):
- The episodic buffer was added in 2000, not 1974.
- "Articulatory loop" is the old name; today it is called articulatory rehearsal.
- The capacity is ~4 chunks, not 7.

### 7. Accuracy notes (mine, not from the video)

- "~7 items" is Miller (1956). Newer research (Cowan, 2001) puts working memory nearer **~4 chunks**. The 3-4 loop sweet spot fits that better.
- The Zeigarnik effect has mixed replication results. Treat it as a craft rule, not proven science.
- "$3.7M VSL" and "$3K → $17K/day" are the speaker's own claims. He said he did not see the $3.7M ad himself (00:21:10).
- He says viewers "don't audit the payload". That is no licence for false claims. Every fact in the payload must still be true (FTC, and the project truth rule).

### 8. How to use this for UGC hooks (15-60 s)

Checklist for each hook:
1. **Open loop in the first line**: the viewer must want the answer.
2. **One Milton pattern**: unspecified noun, cause-effect, or presupposition.
3. **Specific avatar call-out** in their words, then vague where emotion should land.
4. **Juxtaposition** if the product allows it (two things that should not go together).
5. **Close the hook loop at the end**, right before the CTA.
6. Under 30 s: 1 loop (A only). 30-60 s: A + B. 2 min+: A + B + C nested.

Hook templates:
- Unspecified: "Something about your house has been keeping you stuck."
- Transderivational: "You already know which repair you've been putting off."
- Cause-effect: "As you watch this, you'll notice why the first offer is never the real one."
- Presupposition: "Before you decide *when* to sell…" / "When you pick your closing date…"
- Juxtaposition: "I sold an ugly house for a good price." / "The best offer I got was for the worst house on the street."
- Contrarian loop: "Your agent isn't telling you the full cost of listing."

# Part B: Research check


## Executive summary

1. **The Milton Model and "transderivational search" have no direct scientific support** as named techniques. But several of the effects they describe *do* exist under other names: presupposition, self-generated conclusions, the Barnum effect, and fluency. Use the patterns because of that evidence, not because of NLP.
2. **Open loops work, but for a different reason than the call said.** The Zeigarnik "unfinished things are remembered better" claim failed a 2025 meta-analysis. What holds up is the **pull to finish** (Ovsiankina effect) and **curiosity**. Curiosity activates reward areas and improves memory even for other information shown during the curious state (Gruber 2014). That second finding is the real case for putting your payload inside an open loop.
3. **Working memory holds ~4 chunks, not 7.** Keep at most 3-4 loops open.
4. **"Overload them so they don't scrutinise" is half true.** Distraction and story absorption lower counterarguing, which helps **weak** claims and **hurts strong** ones. If your claim is strong and true, let viewers think about it.
5. **Story is the best-supported lever.** A meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes found narrative transportation raises emotion (ρ=.57) and attitudes (ρ=.44) and lowers critical thoughts (ρ=−.20).
6. **For short-form, the key message goes in the first 3 seconds** (TikTok: 63%+ of highest-CTR videos). Curiosity must be **moderate**: vague headlines gained from more detail, and already-specific headlines lost from it (Scientific Reports 2025).
7. **Legal line:** an AI-generated "customer" testimonial is banned by the FTC's 2024 rule. Cash-home-buyer ads also fall under Meta's Housing special ad category (targeting limits).

## 1. Milton Model, NLP and transderivational search: **NONE**

- A systematic review of NLP health interventions included 10 of 1,459 studies. 4 of 5 RCTs showed no significant difference, with high or uncertain risk of bias. Conclusion: "little evidence that NLP interventions improve health-related outcomes" (Sturt et al., 2012, *British Journal of General Practice*) [1].
- A review of 63 studies from ISI-listed journals concluded the evidence does not support NLP's claim to an empirical base (Witkowski, 2010, *Polish Psychological Bulletin*) [2]. The split of supportive vs non-supportive studies is unverified; the abstract was blocked.
- "Transderivational search" is an NLP term (Bandler & Grinder, 1976). Its sources are NLP practitioners only; no peer-reviewed test was found [3].
- **What this means:** do not say "this hacks the subconscious." The individual patterns still earn their place through sections 2-4.

## 2. The three language patterns, checked

### 2a. Presupposition: **MIXED, leaning supportive**
- Question wording changes memory: "smashed" gave a 41 mph mean speed estimate, vs 34 mph for "hit", and more false reports of broken glass (Loftus & Palmer, 1974) [4]. Figures come via a secondary summary.
- A 2021 review of presupposition processing found false *presupposed* content is detected less often than false *asserted* content. In one Loftus study cited there, false presuppositions produced 29.2% memory errors vs 15.6% for false assertions. EEG studies, however, show presuppositions cost *more* processing, not less (*Frontiers in Communication*, 2021) [5].
- One experiment found presuppositions beat assertions **only when the listener accepts them** (*Applied Psycholinguistics*; authors and year unverified) [6].
- **Use:** "When you sell…" / "Once you get your offer…". Keep the presupposed part believable, or it backfires.

### 2b. Vague / unspecified language: **STRONG (Barnum) but with a limit**
- 39 students rated the same generic personality sketch 4.26 out of 5 for how well it fit them (Forer, 1949; widely replicated as the Barnum effect) [7]. Vague statements feel personal.
- Concrete wording feeling "more true" (Hansen & Wänke, 2010) [8] **failed** a preregistered replication: dz = 0.08 and 0.11, CIs crossing zero (Henderson et al., 2019, *Collabra*) [9]. **WEAK.**
- In 8,977 news-headline tests, adding concreteness raised CTR ~5.5% only when the headline was already vague; for already-concrete headlines, more detail *lowered* CTR ~9.9% (Aubin Le Quéré & Matias, 2025, *Scientific Reports*) [10].
- **Use:** Howie's rule matches the data. Be specific in the avatar call-out and vague on the emotion. Aim for a *medium* information level, not a riddle.

### 2c. Cause-effect / "because": **MIXED**
- Langer's copy-machine study: a meaningless "because" raised compliance from .60 to .93 for a **small** request. For a **big** request it did nothing (.24 → .24); only a real reason helped (.42). Small sample (Langer, Blank & Chanowitz, 1978; Table 1 read directly) [11]. A partial replication exists (Folkes, 1985), results unverified [12].
- **Use:** "because" linking works for small asks (watch, comment, tap). For a big decision like selling a house, the reason must be real.

## 3. Self-generated conclusions: **STRONG, but needs involvement**

- People who improvised arguments changed their own views more than people who only heard or read them (Janis & King, 1954) [13].
- Ads that leave the conclusion unstated produced more favourable, more accessible brand attitudes, **under high involvement** (Kardes, 1988, *Journal of Consumer Research*) [14].
- Open-ended ads beat closed ones on attitude, intent and choice (still true after one week) for involved viewers. For uninvolved viewers the difference was small and not significant (Sawyer & Howard, 1991, *Journal of Marketing Research*) [15].
- Self-referencing copy ("you") raised persuasion when arguments were strong. Combined with other elaboration boosters, the effect weakened or reversed (Burnkrant & Unnava, 1995) [16].
- **Use:** let the viewer finish the thought ("You already know what that roof is costing you"), but only **after** the hook has made them care. A scroller has no involvement yet, so the first line must be clear, not an open riddle.

### 3a. Self-referencing (slide at 16:00): **STRONG for memory, conditional for persuasion**

- Relating information to yourself improves memory. A meta-analysis of 129 studies found an average effect size of .45. The effect is smaller when the comparison is "think about another person" (Symons & Johnson, 1997, *Psychological Bulletin*) [50].
- Generating information yourself beats reading it. A meta-analysis of 86 studies found d = .40 (Bertsch et al., 2007, *Memory & Cognition*) [51]. This is memory, not persuasion.
- In ads, self-referencing copy raised recall. Strong arguments counted more under high self-referencing (Burnkrant & Unnava, 1989) [52].
- **Moderate beats extreme:** a moderate increase in self-referencing helped persuasion and an extreme one hurt it. Both effects appeared only in motivated viewers; low-motivation viewers showed no effect (Meyers-Levy & Peracchio, 1996, *Journal of Consumer Research*) [53].
- **Story beats analysis:** narrative self-referencing (imagining yourself using the product) persuades through transportation, and weak arguments stop hurting. Analytical self-referencing ("think how much you'd save") makes argument quality matter more (Escalas, 2007) [34].
- Self-persuasion beat direct persuasion only for highly involved consumers (Bernritter, Müller & van Ooijen, 2017, *European Journal of Marketing*) [54].
- **Backfire warning, ease of retrieval:** people asked for 12 examples rated themselves lower than people asked for 6, because it felt hard (Schwarz et al., 1991) [55]. BMW was rated higher when an ad asked for **1** reason than for **10** (Wänke, Bohner & Jurkowitsch, 1997) [56]. Replication has been questioned [57].
- **Use:**
  - Use one self-reference cue per line ("Picture handing over the keys next Friday"), not a pile of "you, your, imagine yourself".
  - Ask for **one** reason, never "think of all the reasons".
  - Use it after the hook has made the viewer care.

## 4. Open loops: what actually holds

### 4a. Zeigarnik effect: **MIXED / failed as a memory claim**
- Meta-analysis: "no memory advantage for unfinished tasks", but "a general tendency to resume tasks". The Ovsiankina effect is general; the Zeigarnik effect "lacks universal validity" (Ghibellini & Meier, 2025, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) [17].
- **Use:** claim "open loops make people want to finish", never "they remember more".

### 4b. Curiosity / information gap: **STRONG (lab data)**
- Curiosity is a felt deprivation from a perceived gap in knowledge (Loewenstein, 1994, *Psychological Bulletin*) [18].
- Curiosity activated the caudate (a reward area). People paid tokens or waited to get answers, and remembered surprising answers better 1-2 weeks later (Kang et al., 2009, *Psychological Science*) [19].
- During high-curiosity states, memory improved **also for unrelated material shown in the gap** (faces), immediately and a day later (Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath, 2014, *Neuron*) [20].
- **Use:** this is the evidence behind "payload inside the loop". Open the gap, deliver the mechanism *while* it is open, and close it after. Caveat: the incidental material was faces, not ad claims.

### 4c. Working memory limit: **STRONG**
- Miller's 7±2 was "a rough estimate and a rhetorical device"; the real limit is 3-5 chunks (~4) (Cowan, 2001, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*) [21].
- The model: central executive, phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974), plus the episodic buffer (Baddeley, 2000) [22]. Speech memory span ≈ what you can say in ~2 seconds (Baddeley, Thomson & Buchanan, 1975) [23].
- **Use:** max 3-4 loops open at once. Keep spoken lines short: one idea per line.

### 4d. Mystery / delayed reveal: **MIXED, few studies**
- "Mystery ads" (brand revealed at the end) built stronger category → brand memory links than brand-first versions, most for unfamiliar brands (Fazio, Herr & Powell, 1992) [24]. Abstract only.
- Brief, repeated brand appearances ("pulsing") reduced zapping. A brand placed centrally on screen increased avoidance (Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters, 2010, *Marketing Science*; eye tracking, 31 ads, ~2,000 people) [25].
- Surprise and joy hold attention; for joy, how fast it rises matters most for keeping viewers watching (Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters, 2012, *Journal of Marketing Research*) [26].
- **Use:** an unknown local buyer can hold the brand reveal to the close, but flash the name briefly throughout rather than parking a big logo mid-screen.

### 4e. Loop order: staggered vs back-to-back: **no direct test; supported by inference**

- **No controlled experiment** was found that compares staggered or nested loops against open-then-close-immediately in ads or short video.
- The case for the user's rule (never leave the viewer loop-free) comes from curiosity research:
  - Curiosity is a deprivation state that drives people to close a gap (Loewenstein, 1994) [18]. Once the gap closes, the drive ends. That is what back-to-back open/close does after every line.
  - Curiosity peaks when the viewer is about 50% sure of the answer (inverted U, r = .44) (Kang et al., 2009) [19]. Each gap should be half-guessable, not a total riddle.
  - Information shown **during** an open gap is remembered better (Gruber et al., 2014) [20]. That argues for keeping a loop open across the payload and the offer.
  - People will pay, and even accept electric shocks, for information they can't use (FitzGibbon, Lau & Murayama, 2020, review) [58].
- Event order sets the feeling: withholding the outcome gives suspense, showing the outcome first gives curiosity, an unexpected event gives surprise (Brewer & Lichtenstein, 1982) [59]. "Show the result, then rewind" is a curiosity structure.
- Cliffhangers are mixed:
  - Wirz et al. (2022, n = 133): cliffhangers raised arousal but did **not** raise enjoyment or intent to keep watching [60].
  - Hahn, Schibler & Green (2023, ~500 people): cliffhangers encourage continuing [61].
- People do tend to go back and finish open tasks (about 67% vs a 50% baseline, the Ovsiankina effect). Unfinished tasks are **not** remembered better (Ghibellini & Meier, 2025) [17].
- **Use:** follow the user's loop order rule, and treat "staggered beats back-to-back" as a strong hypothesis. Test it on retention curves: compare the 3-second hold and average watch time of two cuts.

### 4f. Working memory model applied to video: **STRONG**

- **The model:** Baddeley & Hitch (1974) proposed three parts: central executive, phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad. Baddeley (2000) added the episodic buffer, a limited store that binds sound, image and long-term memory into one "episode" [62][22].
- **Phonological loop** = a phonological store plus articulatory rehearsal.
  - The store holds about 2 s of speech unless rehearsed. Memory span ≈ what a person can say in about 2 s (Baddeley, Thomson & Buchanan, 1975) [23].
  - "Articulatory loop" was the original 1974 name. It was renamed because storage, not articulation, is the core (Baddeley & Larsen, 2007) [63].
  - Hughes (2025) disputes whether the store exists at all.
- **Central executive:** controls attention and has limited capacity. "No storage of its own" is textbook consensus; the primary text was not verified.
- **Separate channels:** a visual task and a verbal task run together interfere far less than two verbal tasks (Brooks, 1968) [64].
- **Modality effect:** narration plus pictures beats on-screen text plus pictures. Meta-analysis of 43 effects: d = 0.72 (95% CI 0.52-0.92). It is strongest when the video sets the pace, as ads do (Mousavi, Low & Sweller, 1995; Ginns, 2005) [65][66].
- **Redundancy effect:** full on-screen text that repeats the narration **lowered** retention and transfer (Mayer, Heiser & Lonn, 2001) [67]. 2-3 **keywords** on screen improved retention without adding load (Mayer & Johnson, 2008) [68]. Signaling (highlighting key parts) helped, per a meta-analysis (Schneider et al., 2018) [69].
- **Sound on vs off:**
  - "85% of Facebook video is watched without sound" (Digiday, May 2016; publisher data, not Meta) [70].
  - Meta, Nov 2024: "Over 75% of Reels views on Instagram are sound on" (no method given) [71].
- **Use for UGC:**
  1. The voice carries the words and the picture carries the proof. Two channels, so no overload.
  2. Captions: 2-3 keywords per line, not full-sentence subtitles that repeat every word.
  3. One spoken idea per short phrase (~2 s of speech).
  4. One focal action per shot, because the central executive can't track two new things at once.
  5. Build for sound-on, with keyword captions as the silent-viewer fallback.

### 4g. "Open the loop in the first scene" / show the outcome first: **theory strong, ad evidence missing**

- **Order sets the feeling.** Withholding the outcome gives suspense. Showing the outcome first and hiding what led to it gives **curiosity**. An unexpected event gives surprise (Brewer & Lichtenstein, 1982) [59].
- **Spoilers:**
  - Leavitt & Christenfeld (2011) found spoiled stories were enjoyed more [72].
  - Replications failed or reversed: Johnson & Rosenbaum (2015, n = 412) found unspoiled stories "more fun and suspenseful" [73]. Levine et al. (2016) found spoilers reduced enjoyment. One review says the original "has not been independently replicated" [74].
  - **Mixed, leaning negative.**
- **No ad or film experiment** was found showing that a flash-forward opening raises retention. One 2026 tourism-ad study found chronological order improved memory (Wang, Deng & Fang) [75].
- **Use:** open on the **result or a strange moment** (e.g., keys handed over, a sold sign on a run-down house), but hide the **how**. The unanswered "how" is the loop. Do not reveal the mechanism in scene 1. A/B test cold-open vs chronological cuts.

## 5. Scrutiny, overload and story

### 5a. Cognitive load and belief: **MIXED**
- Under load, people accepted false statements and used them. Interrupted subjects recommended "nearly twice as much" prison time when false statements made a crime look worse (Gilbert, Tafarodi & Malone, 1993) [27].
- This replicated only for statements that carry no information when false (Hasson, Simmons & Todorov, 2005) [28]. Other papers argue against the model [29].
- Distraction **raised** persuasion for easy-to-counter messages and **lowered** it for strong ones (Petty, Wells & Brock, 1976) [30].
- **Use:** do not rely on overload. Strong true claim → state it clearly. Loops are for attention, not for sneaking claims past people.

### 5b. Narrative transportation: **STRONG (meta-analysis)**
- Transported readers adopted story-consistent beliefs and spotted fewer "false notes" (Green & Brock, 2000, *JPSP*) [31].
- Meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes from 76 articles. Transportation → affect ρ=.57, attitudes ρ=.44, intentions ρ=.31, beliefs ρ=.26, critical thoughts ρ=−.20. The paper also notes commercial stories may trigger more critical thought (van Laer et al., 2014, *Journal of Consumer Research*) [32].
- Narrative ads build self-brand connection (Escalas, 2004) [33]. When viewers are transported, weak arguments stop hurting persuasion (Escalas, 2007) [34].
- **Use:** story with a character, a setting and visual detail is the strongest tool here. Howie's "stories within stories" is the part with the best evidence.

### 5c. Repetition and fluency: **STRONG**
- Repeated statements gain believability, true or false (Hasher et al., 1977) [35]. Meta-analysis of 51 studies (Dechêne et al., 2010) [36]. Prior knowledge does not fully protect against it (Fazio et al., 2015) [37].
- Rhyming versions of the same claim were judged more accurate (McGlone & Tofighbakhsh, 2000) [38]. Single classic study.
- **Use:** repeat the core promise 2-3 times in different words. A rhythmic tagline helps. This also means repeating a *false* claim is dangerous, so only repeat what is true.

## Key takeaways: the hook playbook

**Line 1 (0-3 s), the hook.** Key message or the gap, stated clearly. Use one of:
- **Juxtaposition:** two things that shouldn't go together. "I sold the ugliest house on my street without fixing a thing."
- **Negative / warning:** backed by the 2.3%-per-negative-word finding. "Don't list your house until you hear this."
- **Contrarian truth:** "Repairs before selling usually lose you money." Only if it is true for your market.
- **Specific avatar call-out:** "If you inherited a house you don't want to deal with…"
- **Avoid:** the question-only hook (slightly negative data), and pure riddles with no information (the 2025 data favours moderate information).

**Lines 2-4, loops.**
- Under 30 s: one loop, closed right before the CTA.
- 30-60 s: two loops.
- 2 min+: A-B-C nesting, 4 open at most.
- **Loop order (user rule):** there are two allowed patterns.
  - Nested: open A → B → C → close C → B → A.
  - Staggered: open A → open B → close A → open C → close B → close C.
  - **Forbidden:** open A → close A → open B → close B (back-to-back open and close). Always have another loop open when you close one.
- Deliver the mechanism *while* the loop is open (curiosity boosts memory for what is shown in the gap).

**Language patterns.**
- Presupposition: "When you get your cash offer…"
- Vague emotion after a specific call-out: "You already know what that house has been costing you."
- A real "because" for the big ask.

**Story.** A named character, a place, one vivid visual detail. Transportation is the best-evidenced lever.

**Repeat** the one true core promise 2-3 times in different words.

**Close A, then a soft or direct CTA.** The platform link does the rest.

**Truth gate.** No invented numbers, no fake customers. Weak claims are exactly what overload "helps", and exactly what gets flagged.
